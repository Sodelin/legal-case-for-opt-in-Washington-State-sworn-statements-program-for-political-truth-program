
# Washington Voluntary Sworn-Format Pilot
## Concept paper for a private-sector pilot using Washington notarial law to separate sworn factual statements from unsworn opinion and analysis

**Draft purpose.** This paper is written for a first-time reader. It is meant to introduce a narrow, voluntary idea: a private-sector interview format in which a willing participant may choose to place a short block of factual claims under a Washington oath-backed notarial format before moving into ordinary opinion, strategy, and analysis.

**What this paper is not.** It is not a claim that a private interview automatically becomes a federal proceeding. It is not a demand that government compel speech. It is not a plan to trap guests, force convictions, or create a national anti-lying regime.

**Bottom line.** The strongest version of the idea is a **Washington-rooted voluntary sworn format** for discrete factual statements. Its first job is to create a visible trust signal and a clean public record. Any criminal-law consequences are secondary and narrower than the signaling function.

---

## 0. Executive summary

This proposal asks whether Washington law can support a **repeatable private-sector sworn format** for long-form interviews with willing public figures, especially politicians. The answer is **probably yes for the format itself**, **plausibly yes for a false-swearing backstop in at least some cases**, and **uncertain for first-degree perjury or aggressive interstate enforcement**.[1][2][3][4]

The proposal is intentionally modest. It is designed to let a willing guest:
1. receive a short set of factual questions in advance,
2. answer those questions in a **sworn factual block** before a properly commissioned Washington notary,
3. make corrections under a controlled protocol,
4. and then continue to an **unsworn commentary block** for opinion, policy judgment, and analysis.

The format is meant to produce three public signals:
- **Sworn factual block**
- **Unsworn commentary**
- **Declined sworn format**

The first claim of the paper is that **every building block already exists** in Washington law. Washington statutes and rules already define notarial acts, remote notarial acts, verification on oath or affirmation, identity proofing, communication-technology requirements, the licensing path for electronic and remote endorsements, and the crimes of perjury and false swearing.[1][2][3][5][6]

The second claim is that this proposal should be presented as a **trust-calibration pilot**, not as a prosecutorial device. The pilot’s central purpose is to encourage sharper separation between:
- fact and opinion,
- sworn certification and ordinary commentary,
- correction and reckless overstatement.

The third claim is that federal and state law matter differently here. The federal perjury statute requires an oath in a case where **federal law authorizes the oath**; the federal false-statements statute covers materially false statements in matters within the jurisdiction of the federal government. By contrast, Washington’s false-swearing statute does not require the same federal-jurisdiction hook and is tied to Washington’s own broad definitions of “oath,” “official proceeding,” and authorized oath administration.[7][8][9]

# Part I. Concept and purpose

## 1. How the idea arose, and what it symbolizes

The motivating problem is not just “politicians lie.” The more specific problem is that long-form interviews often mix several very different kinds of speech into one stream:

- concrete factual claims,
- interpretation,
- prediction,
- rhetoric,
- advocacy,
- memory-based uncertainty,
- and strategic omission.

When all of those categories arrive in one undifferentiated bundle, the audience often has no quick way to tell:
- what the guest is willing to stand behind as fact,
- what the guest is offering only as analysis,
- and what the guest is declining to certify at all.

This proposal symbolizes a narrower and more constructive ambition than “punish lies.” It aims to build a **repeatable private-sector format** that says:

> Here is the factual block.  
> Here is the opinion block.  
> Here is the line between them.

That line is the real innovation.

## 2. What the proposal is, and what it is not

### 2.1 What it is
A private interviewer or media organization offers a guest an **optional Washington sworn factual block** at the start of a long-form interview. If the guest accepts:
- a Washington notary administers an oath or affirmation,
- the guest answers a narrow set of pre-disclosed factual questions,
- the block is recorded and documented,
- corrections are allowed under a defined protocol,
- and the interview then continues into unsworn commentary.

### 2.2 What it is not
This proposal is **not**:
- a demand for federal legislation,
- an attempt to force private media to carry government speech,
- an effort to make all political lying illegal,
- or a scheme designed mainly to create criminal exposure.

Its primary function is **signaling, discipline, and public recordkeeping**.

## 3. Why the pilot should be voluntary

The voluntary design is not just politically safer. It is conceptually better.

A voluntary pilot:
- preserves guest agency,
- avoids a speech-control posture,
- lowers constitutional risk,
- creates a meaningful audience signal from **acceptance, refusal, and correction behavior**,
- and lets the format be tested before anybody talks about legislation.

The pilot should therefore be framed as:
- **opt-in**, not compulsory;
- **clarifying**, not punitive;
- **record-oriented**, not gotcha-oriented.

## 4. Why this is not a trap

A fair pilot should be designed so that the risk of reckless misstatement is minimized. That means:

1. **Questions are given in advance.**
2. Questions are drafted to be **fact-specific and falsifiable**, not vague or rhetorical.
3. The guest is allowed to answer:
   - “I do not know.”
   - “I cannot certify that.”
   - “That is my opinion, not a factual claim.”
   - “I need to correct or qualify that.”
4. The sworn block is kept short.
5. A correction protocol is built in.

This is important because the pilot is supposed to show that the format can improve discourse **without putting willing participants in unreasonable jeopardy**.

## 5. Falsifiable, knowingly false, and materially false

These terms should not be blurred together.

### 5.1 Falsifiable
“Falsifiable” is mostly an epistemic or practical concept, not a statutory element. A question is falsifiable when there is some conceivable record, event, communication, date, or fact pattern that could show the answer true or false.

Example: “Did you send the March 3 email?” is much more falsifiable than “Did you act dishonestly?”

### 5.2 Knowingly false
“Knowingly false” is the key mental-state idea. It asks whether the speaker **knew** the statement was false at the time it was made. Washington’s false-swearing statute uses this knowledge element, and so does Washington’s perjury law.[4]

### 5.3 Materially false
“Materially false” is narrower and heavier. Under Washington law, a “materially false statement” is one that could have affected the course or outcome of the proceeding. Materiality is an element of perjury, not of false swearing.[10][4]

### 5.4 Why the distinction matters here
A private pilot can be built around **knowingly false** statements in a sworn block without pretending that every wrong answer is “material” in the perjury sense. That is one reason the legal backstop for a pilot is more naturally discussed in terms of **false swearing** than first-degree perjury.

## 6. The guest-choice architecture

A clean pilot should make guest choice visible without making refusal defamatory.

Recommended public labels:

- **Sworn Factual Block**
- **Unsworn Commentary**
- **Declined Sworn Format**

Recommended guest options:

1. Accept the sworn block and answer.
2. Accept the sworn block but decline particular questions.
3. Decline the sworn block entirely and proceed only with unsworn commentary.
4. Provide a later written correction.

The audience then sees not just content, but **how the guest chose to engage with factual accountability**.

## 7. Why this may help discourse

The pilot is designed to test four discourse hypotheses:

1. **Precision hypothesis**  
   Guests will separate fact from opinion more clearly when a sworn factual block exists.

2. **Correction hypothesis**  
   Guests will correct or qualify more often and earlier when the format allows clean correction.

3. **Trust-calibration hypothesis**  
   Audiences will better distinguish between strong factual commitment and ordinary commentary.

4. **Nonpartisan usability hypothesis**  
   The format can be offered to any willing participant regardless of ideology.

These are empirical claims. A pilot can test them.

## 8. Suggested pilot metrics

A serious private pilot should measure:

- opt-in rate,
- decline rate,
- number of qualified answers,
- number of “I don’t know” answers,
- number of fact-to-opinion reclassifications,
- correction rate,
- audience trust shift before and after,
- and whether guests or audiences find the format fair.

A research-style report could then ask whether the format improves factual discipline without chilling commentary.

## 9. Proposed correction protocol

This area should be defined carefully because Washington’s retraction statute is real but narrow.

### 9.1 What Washington law says
Washington provides that a person shall not be convicted of perjury or false swearing if the person retracts the false statement **in the course of the same proceeding**, before it becomes manifest that the falsification will be exposed and before the falsification substantially affects the proceeding.[11]

### 9.2 What the pilot should do
Because “same proceeding” could become a disputed question in a private pilot, the pilot should adopt a conservative correction design:

- **Immediate correction during the same session** is best.
- If a correction is needed before publication, the guest should sign a **corrected sworn declaration** before the same notary if feasible.
- If a correction is needed after publication, the original record should remain preserved and a **supplemental correction record** should be appended.

This correction design is a **recommended protocol**, not a claim that every later correction will necessarily trigger the statutory retraction safe harbor.

# Part II. Washington legal chassis, federal comparison, and operational branches

## 10. What RCW and WAC are

Washington uses two core legal containers here:

### 10.1 RCW
**RCW** means the **Revised Code of Washington**. These are Washington’s codified statutes, enacted by the legislature.[12]

### 10.2 WAC
**WAC** means the **Washington Administrative Code**. These are agency rules adopted under statutory authority.[12]

A simple way to explain the difference:
- **RCW = statute book**
- **WAC = agency rulebook**

In this proposal:
- **RCW 42.45** contains the main Washington notary statute,
- **WAC 308-30** contains the Department of Licensing rules for notaries,
- **RCW 9A.72** contains Washington’s perjury and false-swearing crimes,
- and **RCW 10.88** contains Washington’s Uniform Criminal Extradition Act.[1][4][6]

## 11. Why the format is legally possible in Washington

Washington law already recognizes the key pieces of the proposed format.

### 11.1 Authority to perform notarial acts
A Washington notarial officer may perform a notarial act authorized by chapter 42.45 RCW or by other Washington law.[13]

### 11.2 Definition of a notarial act
A “notarial act” includes administering an oath or affirmation and taking a verification on oath or affirmation.[2]

### 11.3 Definition of verification on oath or affirmation
Washington defines “verification on oath or affirmation” as a declaration made by an individual on oath or affirmation before a notarial officer that a statement in a **record** is true.[2]

### 11.4 Record can be tangible or electronic
Washington defines a “record” broadly enough to include information stored in an electronic medium and retrievable in human-perceivable form.[2]

Taken together, those provisions are what make the proposal structurally plausible. The idea is not inventing a new category of act; it is using an existing Washington notarial tool in a new media format.

## 12. In-person branch versus remote branch

### 12.1 In-person branch
If the guest is physically present with the notary:
- the notary performs a traditional notarial act,
- identity is established under the normal Washington rules,
- the guest signs or verifies the record,
- the notary completes the certificate,
- and the act is entered in the notary’s journal.[14]

This is the simplest branch legally.

### 12.2 Remote branch
Washington also allows an electronic records notary public **located in Washington** to perform a notarial act using communication technology for a remotely located individual, if the statutory conditions are met.[5]

The remote branch requires, among other things:
- identity proofing,
- the notary being reasonably able to confirm that the record before the notary is the same record the signer is executing or verifying,
- creation of an audiovisual recording,
- a certificate stating that communication technology was used,
- and retention of the recording for at least ten years.[5]

### 12.3 Why remote is probably the practical pilot branch
For a media pilot, remote is likely the most scalable version because it avoids travel and can reach guests outside Washington, as long as the Washington notary remains physically located in Washington and the remote-notary rules are followed.[5][15]

## 13. What “secure format” should mean here

“Secure format” should not be left vague. For this pilot, it should mean **Washington-compliant remote-notary technology**.

Washington’s remote-notary rules require communication technology that:
- provides synchronous audio-visual feeds of sufficient clarity,
- allows the notary reasonably to confirm the record,
- and provides reasonable security measures to prevent unauthorized access to the live transmission, identity-verification methods, and the electronic record.[6]

Washington’s rules also define “tamper-evident technology” as technology designed to perform electronic notarial acts and display evidence of changes made to an electronic record.[16]

So, in practical terms, “secure format” should mean:
- a compliant remote-notary platform,
- a stable audiovisual connection,
- controlled identity proofing,
- tamper-evident handling of the electronic record,
- and preserved recording plus journal entry.

## 14. How to get a Washington notary, and how to get the endorsements

### 14.1 Baseline Washington notary commission
To obtain a Washington notary public license, an applicant must meet the state’s eligibility requirements, submit a notarized oath of office, provide a signed $10,000 surety bond, and pay the application fee. Washington DOL lists the application fee as **$40**.[17][18]

### 14.2 Electronic records endorsement
A commissioned Washington notary may apply for an **electronic records notary endorsement** by submitting the required application and fee. DOL lists the electronic endorsement fee as **$15**, and the applicant must provide the name of the tamper-evident technology provider within 30 days and before performing the first electronic notarial act.[17][18]

### 14.3 Remote notarial acts endorsement
A Washington electronic-records notary may then apply for a **remote notary endorsement**. DOL’s public instructions say the remote endorsement must be requested separately and that the notary must wait for approval before remotely notarizing signatures. DOL’s instructional PDF states that there is **no fee** for the remote endorsement itself.[15][19]

### 14.4 Practical timeline branches
**Branch A: already-commissioned Washington notary with both endorsements**  
Fastest path. A pilot can be run as soon as scheduling, platform, and protocol are ready.

**Branch B: Washington notary without endorsements**  
The notary must add the electronic endorsement, enroll with a technology provider, then add the remote endorsement, and wait for approval before performing remote acts.[15][18][19]

**Branch C: no existing notary**  
A new person must first obtain a Washington notary commission, then the electronic endorsement, then the remote endorsement if remote acts are needed.[17][18][19]

### 14.5 What if nobody in the project is a lawyer?
A lawyer is not required to **operate** the pilot. A commissioned notary is required for the oath-backed format itself. But a Washington lawyer is strongly advisable to review:
- the exact wording of the declaration,
- the correction protocol,
- the public labeling,
- and any public statements about criminal exposure.

## 15. Federal law versus Washington law: the critical differences

### 15.1 Federal perjury
Federal perjury under 18 U.S.C. § 1621 requires an oath before a competent tribunal, officer, or person **in a case in which federal law authorizes the oath**, plus a material matter the speaker does not believe to be true.[7]

### 15.2 Federal false statements
Federal false statements under 18 U.S.C. § 1001 do **not** require an oath, but they do require that the statement be in a matter within the jurisdiction of the federal government.[8]

### 15.3 Federal “official proceeding”
The federal definition of “official proceeding” in 18 U.S.C. § 1515 is mainly used in obstruction statutes and focuses on proceedings before federal courts, Congress, and federal agencies authorized by law.[9]

### 15.4 Washington perjury
Washington first-degree perjury requires a materially false statement in an **official proceeding** under an oath required or authorized by law.[4]

### 15.5 Washington false swearing
Washington false swearing requires a knowingly false statement under an oath required or authorized by law, but it does **not** require the extra “official proceeding” element.[4]

### 15.6 Why this comparison matters
This pilot should be grounded in **Washington**, not federal law, because:
- the Washington notary is the operating legal anchor,
- Washington law supplies the notarial mechanism,
- Washington defines “oath” and “official proceeding” broadly,
- and Washington false swearing is a more plausible backstop for a private pilot than federal perjury.

## 16. Official meeting, official proceeding, and why the distinction matters

Washington defines “official proceeding” broadly as a proceeding heard before a legislative, judicial, administrative, or other government agency or official authorized to hear evidence under oath, including a **notary** or other person taking testimony or depositions.[10]

That language is unusually favorable to this proposal. It means a Washington notary is not irrelevant window dressing. Washington’s statute expressly mentions notaries in the definition.

But this does **not** automatically settle every question. A cautious reader should distinguish among three propositions:

1. **Certain proposition:**  
   A Washington notary can validly administer the oath and verification act.

2. **Strong but not absolute proposition:**  
   A structured sworn factual block before a Washington notary has a colorable claim to legal seriousness under Washington law.

3. **Uncertain proposition:**  
   Every private media implementation will necessarily be treated by a court as an “official proceeding” for first-degree perjury purposes.

That third step is where caution belongs.

## 17. Case law that helps, and what it does not prove

### 17.1 State v. Lewis
Washington’s Supreme Court said that merely signing an affidavit in the presence of a notary or other official authorized to administer oaths is sufficient to constitute the taking of an oath. The court also rejected a rigid demand for a theatrical ritual.[20]

**Why it helps:** It supports the idea that Washington treats oath formalities pragmatically, not theatrically.

**What it does not prove:** It does not itself decide the full status of a private political-media pilot.

### 17.2 State v. Smith
In *Smith*, the Washington Supreme Court held a prior written statement signed under oath with penalty of perjury before a notary could, under the facts of that case, be used substantively. The opinion shows Washington treating a notarized oath-backed statement outside classic trial testimony as legally meaningful.[21]

**Why it helps:** It reinforces the seriousness of notarized sworn statements outside court.

**What it does not prove:** The court expressly declined to adopt a universal rule for every sworn statement. So *Smith* is supportive, not dispositive.

### 17.3 State v. Otton
In *Otton*, the Washington Supreme Court reaffirmed its approach to prior sworn statements and continued to read “other proceeding” in the evidence context with relative breadth.[22]

**Why it helps:** It strengthens the general proposition that Washington does not confine legal significance only to courtroom testimony.

**What it does not prove:** Like *Smith*, it is not a direct holding on a voluntary media pilot before a notary.

## 18. False swearing versus perjury: the cleaner pilot framing

This proposal should be framed around the following legal hierarchy:

### 18.1 Safest claim
The format can create a **valid Washington oath-backed record**.

### 18.2 Stronger but still careful claim
If a participant knowingly makes a false statement in that sworn record, Washington **false swearing** is the more natural criminal reference point because it does not require the same “official proceeding” showing that first-degree perjury does.[4]

### 18.3 Risky overclaim
“This definitely creates first-degree perjury exposure.”

That overstates what the current authorities establish. The better formulation is:
- **possible perjury argument in some implementations,**
- **more plausible false-swearing discussion,**
- **strongest value in signal and record.**

## 19. Inter-state reach, extra-state guests, and worst-case enforcement

### 19.1 What is clearly true
A Washington electronic-records notary who is physically located in Washington may perform a remote notarial act for a remotely located individual if the conditions in RCW 42.45.280 are met.[5]

### 19.2 What is also clearly true
Washington law gives out-of-state notarial acts certain effects **in Washington** if performed by authorized officers in the other jurisdiction, but that does not automatically mean every other state must treat a Washington pilot the same way for its own criminal-law purposes.[13]

### 19.3 Worst-case enforcement discussion
If a knowingly false sworn statement led to a Washington charge, extradition would not be automatic. Washington’s Uniform Criminal Extradition Act requires formal written demands, charging documents or affidavits, and executive-level action by the relevant governors.[23]

That means the worst-case path is:
1. Washington charge,
2. interstate extradition request,
3. legal challenge and procedural review.

This is precisely why the pilot should **not** be sold as an easy interstate punishment engine.

### 19.4 Why this does not kill the project
The project does not need smooth extradition to be useful. The main value survives even if criminal enforcement is rare:
- visible sworn acceptance,
- visible refusal,
- archived factual certification,
- and cleaner separation of fact from analysis.

## 20. Why the format may still be worth testing even if the criminal edge is narrow

Even if a cautious lawyer later said, “Do not lead with criminal exposure,” the project would still have value as a **trust-format experiment**.

It would still let observers ask:
- Which politicians accept the sworn block?
- Which politicians decline?
- Which politicians correct themselves?
- Does public trust shift when audiences can see the difference between sworn fact and ordinary rhetoric?

That information alone could be valuable.

## 21. Recommended pilot structure

### 21.1 Before the interview
- Draft 5 to 10 factual questions.
- Provide them to the guest in advance.
- Prepare a short written declaration or verification record.
- Confirm the notary’s commission and endorsements.
- Confirm platform compliance for remote acts if remote is used.

### 21.2 During the sworn block
- Verify identity.
- Administer oath or affirmation.
- Confirm the specific record being verified.
- Ask the pre-disclosed factual questions.
- Allow “I do not know,” “I cannot certify that,” and limited qualification.
- Preserve the recording and certificate.

### 21.3 After the sworn block
- Publish the sworn factual block separately from the commentary block.
- Preserve the notarial certificate and, where appropriate, the declaration.
- Log any corrections under the protocol.
- Keep the original and corrected versions clearly labeled.

## 22. Cost and practicality

The format is low-cost relative to most media production. Washington’s public licensing materials indicate:
- application fee for commission: **$40**,
- electronic endorsement fee: **$15**,
- remote endorsement: **no fee listed in DOL’s instructional PDF**,
- and maximum fees a notary may charge include **$15** for taking a verification on oath or affirmation and **$25** for a remote notarial act.[12][18][19]

The largest practical costs are more likely to be:
- staff time,
- technology setup,
- drafting the declaration and protocol,
- and legal review.

That is why this project is unusually pilot-friendly.

## 23. What the proposal can honestly promise

The proposal can honestly promise:
- a repeatable Washington-rooted format,
- a visible signal of willingness to certify factual claims,
- a cleaner line between fact and opinion,
- a preserved record,
- and an empirical test of whether this improves discourse.

It should **not** promise:
- certain convictions,
- automatic perjury exposure,
- automatic extradition,
- or guaranteed legislative adoption.

## 24. Conclusion

The idea is strongest when kept narrow.

A Washington voluntary sworn-format pilot is legally plausible because Washington already supplies:
- the notary commission,
- the electronic and remote endorsement pathway,
- the verification-on-oath mechanism,
- the communication-technology requirements,
- the broad oath definitions,
- and the crimes of false swearing and perjury.[1][2][4][5][6]

The proposal should therefore be presented as follows:

> This is a private-sector pilot that uses existing Washington law to create an optional sworn factual block for willing interview guests. Its first purpose is to improve trust calibration and factual discipline, not to create a national prosecutorial regime.

That is the clearest, most defensible version of the idea.

## 25. Metacognitive review

### What is strong
- The operating legal pieces clearly exist in Washington law.
- The notary/endorsement pathway is real and concrete.
- The distinction between false swearing and perjury is meaningful and useful.
- The proposal’s signaling value does not depend on winning a hard criminal-law argument.

### What remains uncertain
- Whether a court would treat every private implementation as an “official proceeding” for first-degree perjury.
- How aggressively Washington could or would pursue an out-of-state participant in the rare worst-case scenario.
- How audiences and guests would react behaviorally until a real pilot is run.

### What should be lawyer-reviewed
- the exact declaration text,
- the correction protocol,
- statements about criminal exposure,
- and the public-facing labels.

## 26. Secondary reflection

The most important strategic choice is whether to market this as:
1. a law-adjacent accountability mechanism, or
2. a discourse-improvement format.

The evidence presently supports option **2** more strongly than option **1**.

That does not make the legal architecture unimportant. It means the law is best used here as a **credible chassis**, not as the star of the show.
