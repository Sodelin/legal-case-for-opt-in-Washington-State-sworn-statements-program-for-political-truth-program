# Factual evidence and program-precedent notes

Prepared September 5, 2026. This is a targeted verification memo, not a recommendation concerning the proposal, a political-party assessment, or a finding that the proposed format changes campaign behavior.

## Documents compared

Read the supplied behavioral evidence plan (08), legal feasibility brief (11), the pilot PDF's empirical, program-precedent, methods and bibliography sections (09), and pertinent concept-paper passages (01). The behavioral plan discusses mechanisms and hypothetical evaluation stages. The PDF adds original experiments and an existing-program comparison. The feasibility brief explicitly separates notarial authority, criminal consequences and audience effects. These are different scopes, rather than independent studies of the intervention.

None of these project documents reports collected outcome data from the proposed Washington format. The concept paper's description of possible value as a trust-format experiment is a hypothesis, not an empirical result. The supplied feasibility brief says its companion legal review evaluated no audience or accuracy studies of the complete format.

## Existing programs: what the sources establish

Washington's current Fair Campaign Practices Code addresses campaign candor, misrepresentation and fair-election practices. Its filing provision states that failure to subscribe is not a violation of Title 29B RCW. A separate section describes publication of complaints and responses, with specified timing rules; the Commission does not comment on those complaints or responses under that subsection. The chapter does not set out the proposed notarized factual-interview format. These facts establish an existing voluntary code and an administrative publication mechanism, not an estimate of their behavioral effects. [Chapter 390-32 WAC](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=390-32&full=true).

The League of Women Voters of Clark County describes its Fair Campaign Practices Project as inviting all candidates registering with the county elections office to sign a pledge. Its site links a 2026 code, a three-page project packet and a list of signers. Its stated commitments concern issue-focused campaigning and avoiding misrepresentation and unfair attacks. This identifies an actual invitation-and-pledge program. It does not establish acceptance of the separate Washington notarial proposal by any party or organization. The source attributes the project to the League; a county-hosted document alone does not establish county-government administration. [League program page](https://lwvclarkcounty.org/content.aspx?club_id=160612&module_id=514897&page_id=22).

The signer list was accessible. It documents named participation in that pledge, without supplying a comparison group or outcome analysis. No inference about party-level endorsement or reduced false statements follows from the list itself. [League signer list](https://lwvclarkcounty.org/content.aspx?page_id=22&club_id=160612&module_id=525589).

The 2025 county-hosted code was accessible and describes a voluntary promise about campaign conduct. The linked 2026 code and packet could not be fetched in this review. The 2025 file therefore cannot verify the contents of the 2026 packet. [2025 code](https://clark.wa.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/2025-05/2025-code-of-fair-campaign-practices.pdf).

## Empirical findings and their boundaries

These are statistics reported by the research articles, not scores assigned to the proposal, parties, candidates or officials.

| Research | Verified finding | What was not tested |
|---|---|---|
| Zickfeld and colleagues, 2024, systematic meta-analysis of social commitment | The full review included 445 effects from 121 articles and 91,683 participants. The honesty-norm subgroup estimate was Hedges's g = 0.27, 95% CI 0.19–0.36. The authors report publication-bias evidence. The full N belongs to all commitment types, not solely to oath interventions. | The proposed Washington notarial interview, legal sanctions or party adoption. [Author-institution abstract](https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/committed-dis-honesty-a-systematic-meta-analytic-review-of-the-di/). |
| Zickfeld and colleagues, 2025 (online 2024), randomized online megastudy | 21 oath variants plus no oath, with 21,506 US/UK Prolific participants playing an incentivized tax-reporting game. Pooled mean compliance was 86.2% versus 82.3%; the paper reports d = 0.09. Ten variants differed from control after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Compliance was reported income divided by earned income. | A notarial act, actual tax enforcement, campaigning, audience calibration or the effect of criminal exposure. These are experimental-game results. [Original article at author institution](https://www.runi.ac.il/yedion/facultypublications/getfile.ashx?fileType=3&publicationId=8196&username=s.ayal). |
| Zickfeld and colleagues, 2023, seven preregistered studies | The original abstract reports 7,566 participants across the UK, US and Mexico, with an honesty-oath comparison OR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.72–0.88. It reports no clear effect for the combined oath-and-other-person commitment comparison (OR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.86–1.06). | The proposed legal process. The OR is not a percentage reduction in politicians lying. This review verified the abstract and did not obtain the full text. [Original abstract indexed in PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39242767/). |
| Coppock and colleagues, 2023, panel experiments during the 2020 US election | The author-institution abstract describes eight experiments: factual corrections changed factual beliefs, effects diminished with time, and attitude effects were very small. | An oath or notarization intervention, or a guarantee that belief changes alter voting. No numerical effect was independently re-extracted in this review. [Author-institution record](https://isps.yale.edu/resource/conceptual-replication-of-four-key-findings-about-factual-corrections-and-misinformation). |

The 2025 original article separately describes heterogeneity in earlier oath findings, smaller effects after publication-bias adjustments, and a post hoc change to one equivalence-testing threshold. The new megastudy does not resolve every mechanism or external-validity question. The broad meta-analysis and later experiments are related bodies of evidence, not independent estimates that can simply be added together.

The PDF's discussion of politician fact-checking deterrence is directionally qualified: an earlier field experiment and a later conceptual replication concern different contexts and outcomes. The later publisher abstract reports limited evidence of an effect on legislators' Twitter-post accuracy. That is a factual limitation on extrapolation, not a finding that all fact-checking or all candidate commitments have no effect. [Ma and colleagues, original publisher abstract](https://academic.oup.com/hcr/article-abstract/49/3/321/6909031).

The older behavioral plan also cites an internal meta-analysis of accuracy prompts. The publisher record confirms 20 experiments, N = 26,863. An internal meta-analysis is restricted to the investigators' research program; it is not an exhaustive review of all laboratories. Sharing intentions and the factual content of a sworn interview are different outcomes. The precise 10% estimate was not independently extracted here. [Pennycook and Rand, publisher record](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-30073-5).

## Methodological distinctions for human review

The existing project plans describe several different research questions. They would answer different things even if every procedure were executed as described:

| Design described in the documents | Question it can address | Inferential boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Usability/tabletop exercise | Can people follow the steps, understand the notary's role and locate a correction? | Does not identify an effect on campaign truthfulness. |
| Audience randomization with identical substantive content | Does the presentation change interpretation or comprehension? | Cannot show that an oath changed the speaker's factual behavior, because content is held constant. |
| Randomized process or invitation comparison | Does assignment to a process or offer change subsequent observations? | Nonparticipation, crossover and clustered observations affect what can be inferred. An offer effect differs from an effect among those who actually take an oath. |
| Observational comparison of signers and nonsigners | How do these self-selected groups differ? | The difference does not isolate an oath effect; participation can depend on confidence, resources, legal advice or prior behavior. |

These distinctions are methodological analysis of the supplied proposals, not recommendations to adopt, fund or legislate the program.

The proposed format combines preparation, documentary sources, commitment language, recording, an optional oath and correction publication. Without separate variation or a justified comparison, a change cannot be attributed uniquely to notarization. Repeated statements by the same person or event are not independent observations. Researcher-selected claims can change which kinds of errors are observable. Publishing only completed appearances loses information about withdrawals. Audience confidence can change without factual accuracy changing. These are identifiable inference risks; no resulting political score is calculated here.

## 11. Process reflection

This was a targeted source verification informed by the supplied corpus, not an exhaustive systematic review. Searches used article titles, DOIs, official WAC pages and program-owner pages. The 2025 megastudy full text was inspected; several other findings were checked only against original abstracts. The 2026 Clark packet and replication correction remained inaccessible. There was no duplicate study screening, fresh risk-of-bias coding, data reanalysis, or full retraction search covering every cited article. No process-integrity score or policy rating is assigned.

## 12. Inference reflection

Documented existence of a voluntary pledge, a lawful notarial mechanism, a laboratory honesty effect and a measured public response are distinct observations. None establishes the others. This review calculates no pooled effect: the included constructs and outcomes differ. It does not establish the precise Washington format's effectiveness, broad organizational uptake, interstate acceptability, or effects of criminal liability. Evidence about those questions would require observations of the actual process and clearly identified comparison conditions; that is a description of the missing evidence, not a program recommendation.
