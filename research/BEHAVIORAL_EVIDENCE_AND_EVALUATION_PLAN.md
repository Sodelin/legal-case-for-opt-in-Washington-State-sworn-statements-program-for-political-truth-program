# Behavioral evidence and evaluation plan

**Relationship to canonical memorandum:** This file expands the empirical layer. It does not alter the legal conclusions in the canonical memorandum.

## 0. Executive decision brief

The available behavioral evidence supports a pilot, not a verdict. Honesty oaths and pledges produce a small-to-moderate average improvement in honest behavior across heterogeneous experimental literatures. Accuracy prompts can improve sharing discernment. Political fact-checking improves belief accuracy on average, but effects weaken in campaign contexts and under motivated reasoning. Corrections often leave residual influence.

The exact intervention proposed here has not been tested. Its effect may come from multiple ingredients: advance review, accuracy salience, public commitment, legal formality, source transparency, correction structure, or selection into participation. A credible study must separate those ingredients rather than attributing every effect to “the oath.”

### Evidence bands

| Claim | Evidence strength | Current conclusion |
|---|---:|---|
| Honesty commitments can improve honest behavior | Moderate | Meta-analytic average supports testing; substantial heterogeneity and publication-bias concerns |
| Accuracy salience can improve discernment | Moderate | Replicable in platform-sharing experiments; different behavior from political testimony |
| Fact-checking improves political belief accuracy | Moderate | Positive average effect, attenuated by campaign context and prior beliefs |
| Corrections fully erase misinformation | Low / contrary evidence | Continued influence commonly remains |
| This notarial format improves politician accuracy | Very low | No direct evidence located |
| This format improves calibrated public trust | Very low | Must be tested; false-certainty is a core safety outcome |

## 1. Relevant evidence

### 1.1 Honesty-oath and commitment meta-analysis

Zickfeld and colleagues reviewed 20,988 records and retained 445 effect sizes from 121 articles, representing 91,683 participants across 33 countries. Commitment to honesty norms through oaths or pledges increased honest behavior, **Hedges's g = 0.27, 95% CI [0.19, 0.36]**. Commitment to people or groups moved behavior in the opposite direction. The authors reported moderators and evidence of publication bias.

**Interpretation for this project:** A deliberately honesty-directed commitment is a plausible behavioral ingredient. The average effect does not establish effectiveness for politicians, interviews, legal oaths, public audiences, or high-stakes partisan speech.

### 1.2 Accuracy prompts

Pennycook and Rand internally meta-analyzed 20 experiments with 26,863 participants. Accuracy prompts improved sharing discernment, primarily through an approximately 10 percent reduction in intentions to share false headlines relative to control.

**Interpretation:** Salient accuracy goals can change behavior before sharing. The intervention is much lighter than a notarial act, which creates an important comparison condition: a legal ritual may add value, add nothing, or produce adverse pressure beyond a plain accuracy commitment.

### 1.3 Political fact-checking

Walter, Cohen, Holbert, and Morag synthesized 30 studies with 20,963 participants and reported a positive average effect on political belief accuracy, **d = 0.29**. Effects weakened for campaign claims, partial refutations, truth-scale formats, and audiences with stronger preexisting beliefs, ideology, or knowledge.

**Interpretation:** Corrections can help, but political identity and campaign context are hard terrain. A prospective format should not be marketed as a substitute for evidence-based fact-checking.

### 1.4 Continued influence of misinformation

Walter and Tukachinsky synthesized 32 studies with 6,527 participants. Corrections did not fully eliminate misinformation's influence. Corrections performed better when coherent, worldview-compatible, and delivered by the original source, and worse after repetition or delay.

**Interpretation:** The participant's own correction addendum may have special value, but prompt correction and coherent explanation matter. The original record and correction should be linked visibly.

## 2. Causal model

The intervention may operate through at least seven pathways:

1. **accuracy salience:** attention is redirected toward truth before answering;
2. **self-concept commitment:** the participant publicly commits to honesty;
3. **deliberation:** advance questions increase checking and reduce impulsive response;
4. **legal seriousness:** formal process raises perceived consequence;
5. **provenance:** exact wording and source-of-knowledge fields reduce ambiguity;
6. **selection:** more cautious or honest participants may be more willing to opt in;
7. **audience heuristic:** viewers may trust the badge independently of answer quality.

The seventh pathway can create harm. A protocol that increases trust without accuracy is a failure.

## 3. Evaluation stages

### Stage 0: nonpublic tabletop test

Purpose: compliance, timing, comprehension, version control, correction handling, role separation, and privacy.

No public figure and no generalizable audience data are required.

### Stage 1: exploratory usability study

Use a small convenience sample to test:

- whether viewers understand what the notary did and did not certify;
- whether the sworn/unsworn transition is perceptible;
- whether neutral refusal language is read as an accusation;
- whether the correction display is understandable;
- whether the process appears voluntary or coercive.

Report the sample as exploratory and nonrepresentative.

### Stage 2: preregistered randomized audience experiment

Randomly assign substantively identical interview clips to:

1. ordinary interview;
2. a nonlegal accuracy commitment;
3. the voluntary sworn-factual protocol.

Holding content constant isolates the procedural signal. A factorial design can separately manipulate accuracy commitment, notarization, and correction.

### Stage 3: speaker-process study

With consenting participants or trained actors, test whether the format changes:

- factual accuracy;
- revision before finalization;
- appropriate qualification;
- “unknown” and “not recalled” answers;
- response latency;
- evasion;
- correction willingness.

### Stage 4: low-risk field pilot

Use one willing guest after every legal, technical, ethical, and privacy gate is satisfied. Do not begin with accusations of crime, corruption, sex, election misconduct, or classified information.

## 4. Primary outcomes

### 4.1 Accuracy outcome

Objectively coded agreement between the answer and a pre-established evidence packet, using blinded coders and a preregistered rubric.

### 4.2 Calibration outcome

Association between audience confidence and objective answer accuracy. Raw trust is not enough.

### 4.3 Safety/comprehension outcome

Proportion of viewers who correctly reject the statement: “The Washington notary checked and confirmed that the political claim was true.”

This should be co-primary. A strong trust effect paired with poor comprehension is adverse, not successful.

### 4.4 Process outcomes

- revision count;
- qualification count;
- unknown/not-recalled count;
- item-level decline;
- evidentiary-source disclosure;
- immediate correction;
- later correction;
- response completeness;
- evasiveness scored by blinded coders.

### 4.5 Audience outcomes

- perceived honesty;
- perceived competence;
- perceived coercion;
- trust calibration;
- memory for fact/opinion boundary;
- willingness to share;
- perceived partisan fairness;
- inference from decline;
- false belief that “sworn” means “proven.”

## 5. Hypotheses

- **H1:** accuracy commitment and sworn protocol improve factual accuracy relative to ordinary interview.
- **H2:** the sworn protocol increases appropriate qualification and explicit uncertainty.
- **H3:** the sworn protocol increases calibrated trust more than raw trust.
- **H4:** absent a prominent limitation explanation, the sworn protocol increases false truth-certification beliefs.
- **H5:** a source-delivered correction reduces residual belief more than no correction but does not fully erase it.
- **H6:** refusal labels decrease trust even when accompanied by neutral language; the magnitude is an ethical design constraint.
- **H7:** the sworn protocol adds little or no benefit over a plain accuracy commitment after content and selection are controlled.

H4 and H7 are not hostile hypotheses. They test whether the legal layer is necessary or harmful.

## 6. Sample-size planning

Approximate conventional two-group totals for alpha .05 and 80 percent power:

- **d = .20:** about 787 participants;
- **d = .25:** about 505 participants;
- **d = .30:** about 351 participants.

Approximate three-group one-way designs:

- **f = .10:** about 967 participants;
- **f = .15:** about 431 participants;
- **f = .20:** about 244 participants.

These are orientation estimates. The final calculation must reflect the exact model, primary contrast, clustering, exclusions, attrition, multiplicity, and planned interactions.

## 7. Analysis plan

- preregister one primary accuracy outcome and one safety/comprehension outcome;
- preregister exclusions and missing-data rules;
- report intent-to-treat and per-protocol sensitivity analyses;
- blind factual coders to experimental condition;
- report intercoder reliability;
- report effect sizes and confidence intervals;
- distinguish confirmatory from exploratory subgroup analysis;
- correct for multiplicity;
- publish null, adverse, and asymmetric effects;
- retain materials and analysis code in a reproducible archive.

## 8. Sampling and generalizability

A DGG community sample can answer whether the format is understandable and acceptable within that community. It cannot estimate national or Washington-voter effects without a stronger sample design.

Report:

- recruitment channel;
- eligibility;
- political identity;
- prior familiarity with DGG and the featured guest;
- demographic composition;
- attrition and exclusions;
- whether responses were anonymous.

## 9. Human-subjects and ethics

A systematic investigation designed to contribute to generalizable knowledge may require human-subjects review. Obtain an IRB or HRPP determination before randomized or publishable data collection rather than self-declaring exemption.

The ethics review should address:

- participant and viewer consent;
- public-figure versus research-participant roles;
- recording and publication;
- deception, if any;
- reputational and legal risk;
- data security;
- withdrawal limits once content is public;
- compensation;
- political vulnerability and harassment;
- separation between editorial and research decisions.

## 10. Falsification and stop criteria

Reconsider or stop the format if:

- audience false certainty rises more than calibration;
- neutral refusal labels still create a large unsupported dishonesty inference;
- participants become more evasive without greater accuracy;
- the legal ritual adds no benefit over a plain accuracy prompt;
- participants become less willing to correct errors;
- effects are strongly partisan or depend on selective invitations;
- the format deters a broad range of otherwise willing guests;
- viewers mistake notarial validity for evidentiary proof;
- the implementation cannot preserve privacy and notary independence.

## 11. Process-integrity assessment

This evidence review is targeted rather than a preregistered systematic review. It prioritizes meta-analyses relevant to commitment, accuracy, fact-checking, and correction. It does not establish exhaustive coverage, and the underlying studies differ greatly from political interviews.

**Process-integrity score: 70/100, moderate.** The next upgrade is a preregistered systematic search with duplicate screening, coded intervention features, risk-of-bias assessment, and an open extraction file.

## 12. Meta-analytic robustness assessment

The honesty-commitment estimate is directionally encouraging but heterogeneous and subject to publication-bias concerns. The fact-checking literature shows that political context and prior belief reduce effects. The correction literature shows persistent residual influence.

**Robustness verdict:** sufficient to justify a carefully controlled pilot, insufficient to predict real-world effect size or policy benefit.
