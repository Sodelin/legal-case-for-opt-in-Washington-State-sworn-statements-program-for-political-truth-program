# Project origin and design-rationale supplement

**Relationship to canonical memorandum:** This supplement explains why the project exists and how the design evolved. The controlling legal analysis remains [`DGG_WASHINGTON_SWORN_STATEMENT_MEMORANDUM.md`](DGG_WASHINGTON_SWORN_STATEMENT_MEMORANDUM.md). If the two conflict, the canonical memorandum controls.

**Status:** Public research narrative; not legal advice; not a launch authorization.

## 0. Executive decision brief

The project began with a simple question: could a political interviewer ask a willing public official to place factual claims under a real oath before moving into ordinary commentary? The original intuition was that an oath might make lying punishable. Research changed the idea in three important ways.

First, the useful distinction is not merely between truth and lies. It is between **defined factual commitments**, **uncertainty**, **inference**, **prediction**, **opinion**, and **advocacy**. A durable format should make those categories visible.

Second, the project should not depend on perjury, prosecution, or interstate enforcement. Washington false swearing provides a plausible legal background, but the exact private-media application is unresolved. The project succeeds only if the format is valuable when no one is prosecuted.

Third, voluntariness is not a concession. It is the design. A willing guest receives the questions in advance, may qualify or decline, verifies only a fixed factual record, and then speaks freely in an unsworn lane. The audience receives a provenance signal, not a government truth certificate.

The recommended product is therefore a **repeatable private-sector sworn-factual record protocol** with four outputs:

1. an exact, versioned factual record;
2. a visible boundary between certified answers and unsworn commentary;
3. a neutral record of participation, qualification, decline, and correction;
4. empirical evidence about whether the format improves accuracy and calibrated trust.

## 1. The originating problem

Long-form political interviews often place radically different speech acts inside one uninterrupted performance. A guest may move from direct personal knowledge to hearsay, from description to prediction, from policy preference to factual accusation, and from uncertain inference to confident rhetoric without any visible transition.

Traditional fact-checking helps, but it is normally retrospective. The original statement may travel farther than the correction. The audience may remember the claim but not the epistemic status under which it was made. A later rating also asks a third party to classify the statement after the fact, which can itself become a partisan object.

The proposed format asks a prior question instead:

> Which propositions is the speaker willing to identify, delimit, review, and formally attest as factual before ordinary analysis begins?

That question does not prove truth. It changes the informational architecture around the claim.

## 2. The conceptual evolution

### 2.1 From compulsory oath to voluntary format

The early version imagined requiring political officials to swear before media appearances. That path would raise severe constitutional, institutional, and practical objections. The project therefore moved away from compulsory participation, government control of private media, and mandatory warning labels.

The present design is private and optional. The host offers a format. The guest may accept it, accept only some questions, participate only in the ordinary interview, or decline entirely. No state action is needed to create the first pilot.

### 2.2 From “put the interview under oath” to a fixed record

A free-form interview is a poor legal and epistemic object. Questions change, context expands, predictions appear, and ambiguous language accumulates. The safer and more useful object is a short written record containing exact, numbered answers and qualifications.

The live segment is then a ceremony and publication interface around that record. The oath covers what the record says it covers. The rest of the conversation remains unsworn.

### 2.3 From perjury to the false-swearing distinction

Washington first-degree perjury requires a materially false statement in an official proceeding. Whether a private media session would qualify is unresolved. Washington false swearing is a separate gross-misdemeanor offense and does not contain the same materiality or official-proceeding elements.

That distinction makes the proposal more plausible, but it does not make prosecution automatic. Knowledge, oath validity, territorial jurisdiction, proof, charging discretion, constitutional defenses, and interstate process remain separate questions. The legal background gives the commitment seriousness; it should not become the headline.

### 2.4 From punishment to calibrated trust

The durable goal is not “catching” the guest. It is improving the audience's ability to distinguish:

- what the guest says happened;
- what the guest personally knows;
- what the guest learned from records or advisers;
- what the guest infers;
- what the guest predicts;
- what the guest values or advocates;
- what the guest cannot or will not certify.

A successful pilot may produce more “I do not know” answers, more qualifications, or more declined items. Those are not failures. They are information.

## 3. What the format symbolizes

An oath is not a lie detector. It is a socially and legally structured commitment. In this project, its symbolic function is to mark a point at which the participant moves from ordinary persuasion to a narrower statement of present knowledge and belief.

The format communicates three things, and only three things:

1. the participant voluntarily selected the sworn-record lane;
2. the participant reviewed and adopted the exact record through a legally recognized notarial process;
3. the audience can inspect what was and was not included.

It does not communicate that Washington endorsed the claim, that the notary checked the evidence, or that refusal demonstrates dishonesty.

## 4. The desired interview architecture

### Lane A: voluntary sworn factual record

- short and disclosed in advance;
- limited to concrete factual propositions;
- exact answer options and qualifications;
- “unknown,” “not recalled,” and decline remain available;
- completed through a counsel-approved notarial process;
- published with provenance and limitations.

### Lane B: ordinary unsworn interview

- explanation, analysis, inference, prediction, policy, rhetoric, and opinion;
- no implication that the oath carries over;
- ordinary editorial and fact-checking practices continue.

### Lane C: no sworn participation

- guest may still participate in the ordinary interview;
- status described neutrally;
- no truth score and no inference of guilt or deception.

### Lane D: correction and update

- immediate correction is encouraged before completion;
- later correction creates an append-only, dated record;
- the original is preserved;
- a correction does not prove or concede criminal intent;
- a sworn correction requires a new notarial act.

## 5. Why advance disclosure is a feature

The purpose is not to surprise a participant into a legally dangerous answer. Questions should be provided in advance because the project is testing factual commitment, not improvisational memory under stress.

Advance disclosure permits the participant to:

- check records;
- distinguish personal knowledge from staff reporting;
- narrow dates and scope;
- identify privilege or confidentiality concerns;
- consult counsel;
- correct false premises;
- decide whether the answer belongs in the sworn or unsworn lane.

The harder the project works to prevent an accidental false statement, the more credible its stated purpose becomes.

## 6. Why correction is part of the signal

Public trust should not require performative certainty. A correction system can reward responsible updating while preserving the original record.

The public should be able to see:

- the original claim and version;
- the date and content of the correction;
- the source that prompted the correction;
- whether the correction was sworn or unsworn;
- whether related claims changed.

This makes the format more scientific than theatrical. It treats knowledge as corrigible while making silent rewriting impossible.

## 7. Why DGG is a plausible pilot environment

A politically engaged online community can supply rapid feedback, technically competent volunteers, long-form interview infrastructure, and an audience accustomed to source review. It can also produce severe selection, partisan, and social-pressure effects.

Accordingly, a DGG pilot is useful for feasibility and within-community comprehension. It is not automatically representative of Washington voters, national voters, journalists, or public officials. Any audience study should label the sample honestly and separate editorial experimentation from generalizable research.

## 8. Why the project is Washington-first

Washington is the jurisdiction the project originator could responsibly study from lived location and available state materials. Washington also provides a distinctive combination of:

- broad notarial oath authority;
- a verification-on-oath framework;
- remote electronic notarization;
- a false-swearing offense distinct from first-degree perjury;
- a statutory amendment effective January 1, 2027 that expressly adds standalone remote oral-oath authority.

Other states may possess similar components. That does not justify importing Washington conclusions. Each state requires separate analysis of oath authority, criminal elements, remote procedure, territorial jurisdiction, and state constitutional law.

## 9. Relationship to future public policy

The private pilot comes first. A useful sequence is:

1. legal and technical dry run;
2. low-risk, nonlive pilot;
3. audience-comprehension and speaker-process evaluation;
4. publication of benefits, failures, and correction data;
5. only then, discussion with Washington policymakers, regulators, civic organizations, or other media groups.

Possible later public roles could include neutral model forms, public education, research support, or optional official forums. None of those steps is necessary to test the private format, and none should be presumed before evidence exists.

## 10. Success, failure, and stop conditions

The project succeeds if it improves factual precision, makes uncertainty visible, supports correction, and helps audiences understand the limits of both the claim and the notary.

It fails if:

- participants or viewers believe notarization proves truth;
- the threat of prosecution becomes the principal attraction;
- refusal is weaponized as evidence of dishonesty;
- the sworn lane swallows analysis and opinion;
- correction becomes harder because participants fear updating;
- the format is applied selectively by party or ideology;
- legal and technical compliance cannot be verified;
- audience trust rises without corresponding accuracy.

## 11. Process-integrity reflection

This supplement reconstructs the project's conceptual development rather than claiming new legal authority. Its primary contribution is provenance: it records which early assumptions were abandoned and why. The legal propositions should be checked against the canonical memorandum and source ledger.

## 12. Robustness reflection

The strongest version of the project remains coherent after deleting every promise of prosecution. The weakest version depends on the audience believing that a dramatic criminal consequence is imminent. Future design choices should be judged by that counterfactual.

## 13. Approved one-paragraph description

> The project proposes a private, voluntary interview format in which a willing public figure reviews and verifies a short, pre-disclosed factual-answer record before a qualified Washington notary, then moves into clearly unsworn analysis and opinion. The notary verifies the legal act and identity, not factual truth. The format is designed to make factual commitment, uncertainty, decline, and correction more visible. Washington false-swearing law supplies a possible legal background, but the project does not promise perjury, prosecution, jurisdiction, or extradition.
